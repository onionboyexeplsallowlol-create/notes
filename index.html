Best idea: AshPure AshPure would use processed fly ash to make porous ceramic filters that clean polluted water.
Instead of storing ash in a pond, the ash would be:
Dried and tested.
Mixed with clay or another safe ceramic material.
Shaped into a porous filter.
Heated in a controlled industrial kiln.
Installed in water-treatment systems.
The tiny pores allow water to pass through while trapping suspended particles. The surface could also be modified to remove some heavy metals or other pollutants. Ash pond waste becomes a water-cleaning filter.
Research has explored fly ash in ceramic membranes and reported mechanically stable, porous filters suitable for filtration applications. Coal ash is rich in silica and alumina, which are useful in ceramics, glass-ceramics, geopolymers, zeolites, and filtration materials. What could it replace? AshPure could replace or reduce the need for: Conventional ceramic filter material.
Some clay used in water-filter production.
Part of the silica or alumina raw material.
Some activated filter media, depending on the treatment.
Disposable plastic filter cartridges.
It would not replace every water filter. The filter would need to be designed specifically for the pollutant being removed.
Why it is more innovative Bricks and cement reuse ash as construction material. AshPure turns the same waste into a tool for solving water pollution.
It connects two major environmental problems:
Coal ash ponds can leak pollutants into groundwater.
Polluted water needs affordable treatment.

Exactly. AshPure addresses two major environmental problems at the same time: Coal-ash pollution: It removes ash from storage ponds, reducing the risk of leaks and groundwater contamination. Water pollution: It turns the ash into ceramic filters that help clean contaminated water. The idea is: Coal-ash waste → ceramic filter → cleaner water It also supports additional benefits: Reduces the need to mine new ceramic materials. Reuses industrial waste. Supports cleaner water and sanitation. Creates a potentially low-cost treatment material. Connects climate action with sustainable infrastructure. A good presentation sentence would be: “AshPure hits two birds with one stone by removing hazardous coal ash from storage ponds and transforming it into a useful material for cleaning polluted water.” For a formal science presentation, you could say “solves two connected environmental problems” instead of “hits two birds with one stone.” AshPure is made by turning tested fly ash into a porous ceramic membrane. It is not something to manufacture from raw ash pond material at home because coal ash can contain heavy metals and must be professionally tested first. Ingredients A real AshPure membrane could contain: Processed coal fly ash: main raw material. Clay or natural sand: adds structure and helps the membrane hold its shape. Pore-forming material: sawdust, starch, dextrin, or sugar waste. It burns away during heating and leaves tiny pores. Binder: cellulose, carboxymethyl cellulose, or another temporary binder to hold the wet mixture together. Water: makes the mixture into a paste. Optional treatment layer: activated carbon, zeolite, titanium dioxide, or another material aimed at a particular pollutant. Ceramic membrane production generally involves making a paste, shaping it, drying it, and sintering it at high temperature. Research has used fly ash with natural sand, clay, pore formers, and binders to produce porous filtration membranes. How it is made industrially Collect and test the ash. The ash is checked for heavy metals, radioactivity, and chemical composition. Dry and grind it. Large particles and impurities are removed, and the ash is ground into a fine powder. Mix the ingredients. The ash is mixed with clay or sand, a pore-forming material, binder, and water. Shape the filter. The paste is pressed into a disk, tube, plate, or honeycomb shape. Dry it slowly. Water is removed carefully so the piece does not crack. Heat it in a kiln. The binder and pore former burn away, leaving connected pores. The ash and clay become a hard ceramic. Add an optional filter coating. Activated carbon or zeolite can be added if the filter needs to remove dyes, odors, or some dissolved pollutants. Test the final filter. Engineers measure its pore size, strength, flow rate, pollutant removal, and whether harmful substances leak from the ash. Studies have produced fly-ash ceramic membranes by pressing the mixture and heating it at approximately 800–1,150°C. One fly-ash-and-clay study produced a microfiltration membrane with an average pore size of about 0.6 micrometres. Safe school model Do not use real ash from an ash pond. Use safe substitutes: Clay. Fine sand. Charcoal. Starch or sawdust. Water. Mix the clay and sand with a small amount of starch or sawdust, shape it into a small disk, and let it dry. If your school has a supervised ceramics kiln, it could be fired properly. Otherwise, present it as a model of the manufacturing process, not a real drinking-water filter. For a simple demonstration, place the dry porous disk in a funnel and pour colored water through it. Compare the result with water passed through ordinary sand. Important limitation AshPure would mainly remove particles and some larger contaminants. It would not automatically make water safe to drink. Dissolved salts, microbes, heavy metals, and chemicals may pass through unless the membrane has additional treatment layers. Simple explanation AshPure mixes tested coal ash with clay, sand, a temporary pore-former, and water. The mixture is shaped, dried, and heated until it becomes a hard ceramic full of tiny holes. Water passes through the holes, while some pollution is trapped. Coal ash is extracted from the slurry through a process called dewatering. The goal is to separate the solid ash from the water. Simple process Pump the slurry out of the ash pond. Let it settle in a thickening tank. The heavier ash sinks to the bottom, and clearer water rises to the top. Remove the water from the top and send it to a treatment system. It must not simply be released into a river or groundwater. Press the thick ash through a belt filter press or filter press. The filter cloth lets water pass through but holds the ash. Collect the solid ash cake. Dry and test the ash before using it to make AshPure ceramic filters. A belt filter press removes water by squeezing wet material between moving filter belts, producing a solid material called filter cake. What happens to the water? The separated water may still contain dissolved pollutants, so it must be treated. It could be: Cleaned and reused in the power plant. Sent through a wastewater-treatment system. Carefully discharged only after meeting environmental standards. Real ash-pond closure projects commonly include dewatering, water treatment, solids handling, stabilization, and long-term monitoring. Potential judge questions and answers

1. What is AshPure? Answer: AshPure is a proposed ceramic water filter made partly from processed coal ash. It turns waste from coal-ash ponds into a useful material for treating polluted water. 2. What is an ash pond? Answer: An ash pond is a large man-made basin where coal ash from power stations is mixed with water and stored as slurry. 3. How does coal ash reach the pond? Answer: Coal is burned to produce electricity. The leftover ash is mixed with water and pumped through pipes into the pond, where the solid ash settles at the bottom. 4. Why are ash ponds a problem? Answer: Ash ponds take up land and can leak pollutants into groundwater. If their walls fail, ash slurry can spread into nearby land and rivers. 5. How does AshPure solve two problems? Answer: It removes coal ash from storage ponds and converts it into a ceramic material that helps clean polluted water. 6. How is ash extracted from the slurry? Answer: The slurry is pumped into a settling tank. The ash sinks, water rises, and the remaining wet ash is pressed through a filter press to make a solid ash cake. 7. What happens to the separated water? Answer: It is collected, treated, tested, and either reused or released only if it meets environmental standards. It must not be discharged untreated. 8. What is AshPure made of? Answer: A full-scale filter could contain processed fly ash, clay or sand, a temporary pore-forming material such as sawdust or starch, water, and a binder. Optional layers could include activated carbon or zeolite. 9. Why does it need pores? Answer: The pores are tiny pathways that allow water to pass through. They also provide surface area where particles and some pollutants can be trapped. 10. How is it made? Answer: The ingredients are mixed into a paste, shaped into a disk or tube, dried, and heated in a kiln. During heating, the temporary pore-former burns away and leaves connected pores. 11. What can it remove from water? Answer: It can mainly help remove suspended particles and, depending on its design, some dyes, phosphate, metals, or organic pollutants. It will not remove every pollutant automatically. 12. Can it make drinking water? Answer: Not automatically. The filter would need certified testing for microorganisms, heavy metals, salts, and other contaminants before it could be used for drinking water. 13. Could the ash poison the water? Answer: It could if untreated ash is used. Coal ash may contain heavy metals, so the ash must be tested, cleaned, and treated before making the filter. The finished filter must also pass leaching tests. 14. How do you prevent pollutants from leaking out? Answer: The ash can be washed or pre-treated, then locked into a fired ceramic structure. The finished membrane must be tested by soaking it in water and measuring whether pollutants are released. 15. Why not just use normal ceramic? Answer: Normal ceramic requires new clay and mineral resources. AshPure reuses an industrial waste material while creating a product for water treatment. 16. Why not use activated carbon? Answer: Activated carbon is useful, but it can be expensive and often needs to be replaced. AshPure could provide a lower-cost porous support, and activated carbon or zeolite could be added as an extra layer. 17. Is AshPure biodegradable? Answer: No. AshPure is designed to be durable rather than biodegradable. Its sustainability comes from reusing waste, reducing new raw-material extraction, and potentially lowering the cost and environmental impact of water treatment. 18. Is it cheaper than ordinary filters? Answer: It could be cheaper if ash is locally available and processing costs are controlled. However, kiln heating, testing, transport, and pollution treatment add costs. Ceramic membranes can have high manufacturing costs because they require expensive equipment and high-temperature sintering. 19. Can it work on a large scale? Answer: Potentially, yes. Large filters could be installed in wastewater-treatment plants, factories, drainage systems, or desalination pre-treatment systems. Scaling would require consistent ash quality, industrial kilns, safety certification, and regular cleaning. 20. What is the biggest limitation? Answer: The biggest limitation is safety. Coal ash varies between power plants and may contain harmful substances. Every batch must be chemically tested before it is used in a water-treatment product. 21. What is your control experiment? Answer: I would compare ordinary sand filtration with the AshPure material. Both filters would receive the same amount of colored water, and I would compare the color, clarity, flow rate, and amount of water filtered. 22. What materials can you use for the school model? Answer: I would not use real ash-pond material. I would use clay, sand, charcoal, and starch or sawdust as safe substitutes, then explain that the real product would use tested coal ash. 23. What is your hypothesis? Answer: My hypothesis is that a porous ceramic made from processed ash and clay will filter suspended particles and some color from water while reusing coal-ash waste. 24. What makes your project innovative? Answer: It connects two waste problems. Instead of treating coal ash only as a disposal problem, it converts it into a water-treatment material that can help address water pollution. 25. What SDGs does it support? Answer: It supports: SDG 6: Clean Water and Sanitation. SDG 9: Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure. SDG 11: Sustainable Cities and Communities. SDG 12: Responsible Consumption and Production. SDG 13: Climate Action. 26. Does it reduce climate change? Answer: Indirectly. It does not capture carbon dioxide directly, but it reduces waste storage, reuses industrial materials, and may reduce the need to manufacture new ceramic filter materials. The strongest direct benefits are waste reduction and cleaner water. 27. What happens when the filter becomes full? Answer: It would be removed and cleaned or replaced. The used filter would need to be handled as potentially contaminated waste and tested before disposal or recycling. 28. Can it replace every type of water filter? Answer: No. It is best described as a ceramic filtration platform. Different coatings or additional layers would be needed for different pollutants. 29. What is the final product? Answer: The final product could be a porous ceramic disk, tube, plate, or honeycomb cartridge made partly from coal ash. 30. What would you improve in the future? Answer: I would test different ash-to-clay ratios, measure pore size, add activated carbon or zeolite, test heavy-metal leaching, improve the filter’s strength, and design a replaceable cartridge for industrial water-treatment systems.
